Summary:

 The Willows provides a respite service for adults with learning disabilities and physical needs.

 People’s well-being outcomes require improvement. Staff treat people with dignity and respect and
 work as a familiar, close-knit team who know individual needs and preferences well. Relatives told
 us people return from respite happy and are supported to access the community. However, there is
 limited evidence that people’s voices, choices and meaningful engagement are consistently
 promoted, including the involvement of their representatives.

 Care and support require improvement. Assessments of needs and risks are not consistently
 completed, personal plans are not routinely reviewed, and handover arrangements before respite
 stays are not sufficiently robust. This limits the availability of up-to-date information for staff and
 increases the risk that people do not consistently receive good quality, personalised care.
 The environment is good. It is safe, generally accessible and well managed. Hazards are identified

Findings:

        Well-being                 Requires Improvement



 People are treated with dignity and respect. We observed kind, caring and respectful interactions
 between staff and people using the service. Staff including the manager are very familiar with
 people  using  the  service  and  responsive  to  their  immediate  needs.  People  and  their
 representatives consisting of their relatives, told us they experience positive respite outcomes,
 including access to the community and activities they enjoy. We saw people being supported into
 the community during our visit. Relatives told us that, people returned home from respite content
 and settled, and staff kept families informed during significant events.

 Improvement is required to ensure people are consistently supported to exercise choice and control
 in their day-to-day lives, and to make informed decisions. We found limited and inconsistent
 evidence of meaningful activity and engagement. Communication and information sharing tools to
 document people’s experiences requires strengthening. There was limited evidence that people’s
 views were captured, reviewed or used to inform care planning between respite stays. We
 observed that changes to routine and the environment affected one person’s ability to feel at ease
 and in control, indicating that individual needs and known triggers were not sufficiently embedded
 into day-to-day practice. This reduces people’s voice, control and influence over their care.

 People are supported to maintain meaningful relationships with friends and other important people,
 as far as possible. We found a good matching process, with people supported by familiar key staff
 with whom they have developed relationships over a number of years. The staff team is small and
 consistent, and managers demonstrate a good understanding  of people’s preferences and
 personalities. Some relatives told us their family member looked forward to respite stays to see
 their key worker, describing these relationships as familiar and similar to friendships.

 The provider prioritises people’s well-being when evaluating whether the environment meets their
 needs and have agreed additional funding to adapt the kitchen and garden to make  it more
 accessible for people to promote well-being outcomes.

 While there were examples of positive practice, this was not consistently experienced. Variability in
 how choice was offered, how people were engaged, and how people and their representatives’
 views were listened to meant that people did not always experience the same quality of care and
 support. This has been identified as an Area for Improvement, and we expect the provider to take
 action  to  ensure  approaches  to  choice, engagement and  listening  to  people and  their
 representatives are consistently embedded.

       Care & Support            Requires Improvement




People are protected from abuse and neglect and feel safe in the service. The environment is
secure. The manager works closely with multi-agency professionals when concerns arise, including
safeguarding teams, and seeks timely advice to manage risks appropriately.

Medicines are managed safely and consistently. We found medication arrangements were informal
and unreliable, with staff relying on verbal or handwritten information from families. During the
inspection, the provider took immediate action and introduced a formal medication arrangement
and recording system. This has improved the accuracy and reliability of medicines management.

The provider had not completed provider assessments for people using the service. This resulted in
incomplete and inconsistent information, particularly for people with complex needs, and limited the
use of specialist knowledge to inform care. We found one person attends a specialist residential
education placement and makes positive progress in life skills with specialist staff. Relevant reports
and assessments were available but had not been incorporated into care planning. This limits
continuity of support during respite stays. This has been identified as an Area for Improvement, and
we expect the provider to take action.

Staff deliver care with kindness and compassion. However, key elements of safe and effective care
are not consistently  in place. We  identified gaps  in assessment, care planning and review
processes. These gaps limit the provider’s understanding of people’s needs, risks, communication
methods and support strategies, and reduce assurance that care is consistently personalised and
safe. People and their representatives are not routinely involved in reviews of personal plans and
do not consistently have access to them to confirm accuracy. Handover arrangements before and
after respite stays relied largely on verbal and informal processes. In some cases, people arrived
without full or reliable information about recent changes to their care or support needs. This leads
to avoidable inconsistencies in care delivery and increases the risk that people’s needs are not met
promptly or appropriately. The provider took immediate action to ensure reviews and care plans are
more robust. During the inspection, the provider introduced a formal review and handover
document. This action has reduced risk by improving the transfer of information between respite
stays. However, at the time of inspection, there was limited evidence that new arrangements are
consistently embedded or that people and  their representatives are  fully involved  in review
processes.

       Environment                         Good



People live in a safe and welcoming environment that supports their well-being and day-to-day
needs. The service is located within the local community and provides a homely atmosphere, which
helps people feel connected to their neighbourhood. Staff follow clear sign-in and sign-out
procedures and monitor who enters and leaves the service. This supports people’s safety while
maintaining a relaxed, non-institutional environment.

The indoor environment is accessible and supports people to move around safely. Ground-floor
environment enables people to access shared areas with ease. The environment is clean, orderly
and functional. While some areas would benefit from further enhancement to improve comfort and
homeliness, this does not detract from people’s safety or ability to use the space. Bedrooms
provide people with private space, although limited personalisation reduces opportunities for people
to express their individuality. Bathrooms remain functional; however, modernisation would further
enhance people’s experience.

The garden provides attractive views of surrounding farmland and open land, which contributes
positively to people’s emotional well-being. Some areas of the garden are accessible and enable
people to spend time outdoors. The provider told us they will further adapt the garden to ensure all
areas are accessible for people. Where environmental hazards were identified during the
inspection, including unsecured furniture and garden risks, the provider addressed these promptly.
This reduced risk and demonstrated effective management of the environment.

Required health and safety checks are in place and up to date, including legionella management,
portable appliance testing, fire safety equipment, fire drills and Personal Emergency Evacuation
Plans. These arrangements support people’s safety and ensure staff understand how to respond in
an emergency.

The kitchen is accessible and supports daily living activities. It is safe and usable for its current
purpose. While improvements would further enhance people’s well-being and opportunities to
develop life skills, current arrangements do not compromise safety.

Overall, the environment supports positive outcomes for people because it is safe, generally
accessible and well managed. Hazards are identified and addressed promptly. Although some
areas would benefit from further enhancement, these do not adversely affect people’s safety or
overall experience.

       Leadership & Management   Requires Improvement



Leadership and management arrangements are not sufficiently effective in ensuring consistent
compliance, quality and safe outcomes for people. Gaps in practices within leadership and
governance underpin many of the issues identified during the inspection.

We identified multiple instances of gaps in governance records, including safeguarding oversight,
assessment and review processes, notifications and quality assurance. Records were incomplete
and inconsistent. They did not reliably demonstrate that people’s needs were assessed,
understood, monitored or reviewed in line with regulatory requirements. This reduced management
oversight and limited assurance that people consistently receive personalised, safe and appropriate
care. Records do not support effective monitoring of themes, patterns or learning at organisational
level. In addition, not all safeguarding matters and incidents had been notified to CIW as required,
which limited effective regulatory oversight.

Quality assurance arrangements lack sufficient evaluative analysis. The most recent Quality of
Care Review identified areas of non-compliance; however, it is largely descriptive. It does not
consistently analyse why issues have occurred, identify root causes, or demonstrate learning and
improvement over time. Action plans lack clear ownership, timescales and measurable outcomes,
which reduces their effectiveness. Several issues identified through internal monitoring were not
addressed in a timely way. As a result, issues were repeated and opportunities to improve people’s
experiences and outcomes were missed.

People achieve their personal outcomes because the service provider makes sure there are
enough suitably qualified and trained staff to deliver quality care and support. Most staff are
registered with Social Care Wales and receive regular supervision and attend team meetings,
which supports communication and oversight of day-to-day practice. Training provision is generally
appropriate and reflects the needs of people using the service, including those with more complex
needs. Recruitment arrangements are safe and robust, with appropriate pre-employment checks
completed, including Disclosure and Barring Service checks and ongoing monitoring.


Leaders were open and transparent during the inspection and acknowledged gaps in compliance
and areas requiring improvement. The Responsible Individual carries out regular visits and is able
to identify areas for improvement through these arrangements. However, governance, quality
assurance and oversight systems have not been effective in ensuring concerns are addressed in a
timely and sustainable way.

Outcomes for people require improvement under the Leadership and Management theme because
governance, records management, quality assurance and regulatory oversight arrangements are
not sufficiently robust. While risks were responded to when identified and people were not exposed
to sustained harm, leadership systems do not yet consistently drive compliance and improved
outcomes yet. A new Registered Manager has been appointed but had not commenced at the time

Areas identified for improvement
Where we identify Areas for Improvement but we have not found outcomes for people to be at
immediate or significant risk, we discuss these with the provider. We expect the provider to take
action and we will follow this up at the next inspection.


Where we find outcomes for people require significant improvement and/or there is risk to
people’s well-being we identify areas for Priority Action. In these circumstances we issue a
Priority Action Notice(s) to the Provider, and they must take immediate steps to make
improvements. We will inspect again within six months to check improvements have been made
and outcomes for people have improved.
