People at Haulfre Residential Care Home experience excellent well-being. They feel safe, listened
to and able to make everyday choices. Care staff treat people with warmth and respect and support
them to take part in activities, enjoy home-cooked meals and stay connected to their community,
family and friends. Haulfre excels at promoting community engagement.

People receive good care and support. Personal plans are clear, up to date and reflect each
person’s needs, preferences and routines. Care staff recognise changes in people’s health and
contact professionals quickly. They record advice clearly and follow it. Medication is administered
safely. Care staff understand safeguarding, Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards (DoLS) and
risk-management procedures and act on them.

People live in a good environment that promotes comfort and independence. The home is clean,
welcoming and benefits from recent improvements. Some areas still need decoration or repair, but
these issues present a low risk to people and the provider have improvement plans in place.

Leadership and management are good. The responsible individual and manager maintain good
oversight and respond well to feedback. Care staff feel supported, work well as a team and
continue to provide consistent care despite recruitment pressures. Quality assurance systems are
effective and help maintain good outcomes for people.

Findings:

        Well-being                                Excellent



 People living at Haulfre experience excellent well-being. They tell us they feel safe, respected, and
 are able to make everyday choices about their daily lives. We observed warm, relaxed interactions
 where care staff listen to people, encourage independence, and support their individual
 preferences. People describe Haulfre as friendly and comfortable, and some say it feels like
 “home.” This shows the service promotes people’s rights, dignity, and sense of belonging.

 The care home provides a wide range of meaningful activities that support people’s emotional,
 social, and physical well-being. Activities include crafts, baking, quizzes, gentle music sessions,
 church services, themed evenings and pub-style events. People and care staff also work together
 to decorate the veranda for seasonal celebrations, helping create a vibrant and welcoming
 environment. Entertainment is an important part of life at the home. People enjoy regular visits from
 musicians, including well-known Welsh performers Dafydd Iwan and John ac Alun, whose sessions
 create lively and memorable experiences. Care staff also supported one person, who is a lifelong
 fan of Dafydd Iwan, to meet him in person. These personalised opportunities show how care staff
 understand what matters to each person and go beyond routine care.

 The home demonstrates exceptional community engagement. People take part in local events such
 as the Llangoed Flower Show and the annual tractor rally, and enjoy outings to Penmon Point, local
 restaurants, and shops. A recent intergenerational project with Dawns i Bawb and local school
 children, based on “The Very Hungry Caterpillar,” offered several weeks of shared creativity and
 ended with a performance enjoyed by everyone.

 Relationships within the home are strong and genuine. People describe feeling part of a family.
 People were recently invited to the weddings of two care staff members, showing mutual respect
 and the depth of relationships. Relatives say they feel welcomed, valued, and well informed about
 their loved one’s care. People maintain regular contact with family and friends, and care staff
 actively support visits and communication.

 People receive support in Welsh or English according to their preference. Many care staff speak
 Welsh, and others can hold basic conversations. People confirm their language choices are
 respected. The Welsh language and culture is fully promoted and integrated into the culture of the
 service.

       Care & Support                       Good



People living at Haulfre receive care and support that promotes their well-being, protects their
rights, and helps them achieve their personal outcomes. Personal plans are clear, person-centred,
and up to date. They reflect what matters to each person, including their routines, preferences,
cultural needs, communication requirements, health conditions and identified risks. Care staff
involve people, and where appropriate their families or representatives, in assessments and
reviews. This ensures care and support remains relevant, safe and focused on each individual.

The service supports people to maintain their physical and emotional well-being. Care staff
recognise changes in people’s needs and contact health and social care professionals promptly.
Records show good communication with GPs, district nurses, mental health teams, and other
specialists. People benefit from home-cooked meals and are offered choices. Dietary needs and
specialist requirements are understood and met. Care staff encourage good hydration and monitor
weight and nutritional intake where needed. People who require support with mobility, continence,
skin care or oral health receive this sensitively and with respect.

Medication arrangements are generally safe and well managed, and improvements have been
made since the last inspection. Care staff follow the provider’s medication policy, complete
medication administration records accurately, and undertake regular audits. We noted that a
medication fridge was unlocked and this does not meet safe storage requirements. We raised this
immediately with management, who confirmed the issue had been addressed. Despite this, care
staff demonstrated competent and safe practice in administering medicines.

Safeguarding arrangements are strong. People told us they feel safe and are confident speaking
with care staff or the manager if something worries them. Care staff understand safeguarding
procedures, can identify the different types of abuse, and know how to report concerns. They are
familiar with whistleblowing processes and understand their duty to act in the best interests of the
people they support. Records show the provider responds promptly to safeguarding matters and
works closely with the local authority and other professionals. DoLS are applied for when needed,
with assessments and renewals monitored appropriately. Care staff understand any conditions in
place and how these affect the person’s care.

Interactions between care staff and people are warm, respectful and person-centred. People
consistently told us that care staff treat them kindly and support them in ways that make sense to
them. The service provides effective, safe and compassionate care that supports people to achieve
positive outcomes.

       Environment                         Good


People live in an environment that supports their well-being and enables them to achieve their
personal outcomes. The home is warm, welcoming, and generally well maintained. Communal
areas are clean, comfortable and arranged in a way that helps people relax and engage with
others. People told us the home feels homely, and during the inspection we observed them
spending time in the lounges and veranda areas, enjoying the views and chatting with care staff.

Bedrooms are spacious and personalised, with people able to bring their own furniture and
belongings if they wish. This supports a sense of identity and comfort. Most bedrooms are clean,
tidy, and well presented. Care staff respect people’s privacy and knock before entering rooms.
People told us they can rest, spend time alone, or see visitors in private when they choose.

The home has made improvements to the building since the previous inspection. Replacement
double-glazing has been fitted on the veranda areas, and the provider continues to invest in repairs
and maintenance. The leadership team has clear oversight of building issues and works closely
with the local authority’s property department. Routine checks and servicing of equipment are
carried out, including PAT testing, gas safety checks, fire equipment servicing and water
temperature monitoring. Records show these checks are up to date. Some areas of the
environment require continued attention, including decoration in parts of the upstairs corridor,
replacement of worn fixtures, and improved storage in specific rooms. During inspection, we
brought these issues to the manager’s attention, and they provided assurance that work is
scheduled or underway. These matters have low impact on people and do not compromise safety.
The service has a clear plan for addressing these areas.

Fire safety arrangements are in place. The home completes weekly alarm tests, maintains
evacuation equipment, and holds individual Personal Emergency Evacuation Plans (PEEPs) for
people. Recent fire risk assessment recommendations are being actioned. Care staff understand
evacuation procedures and can describe their roles.

People can access outdoor areas, which offer pleasant views and space for fresh air. While some
garden furniture requires repainting, the outdoor areas remain accessible and used by people. The
Active Offer is evident in bilingual signage around the building and through care staff who can
communicate in Welsh when preferred.





       Leadership & Management               Good



                                 Page 6 of 9

--- PAGE 7 ---
Leadership and management at the service are good, and the arrangements in place support
people to experience safe, consistent, and positive care. The Responsible Individual (RI) maintains
strong oversight through regular monitoring visits, reviewing records, speaking with people and
care staff, and checking the environment. These visits follow the regulations and demonstrate a
clear commitment to monitoring quality. The RI’s reports are detailed and highlight what is working
well, alongside actions required to maintain or improve standards. This gives the service a reliable
structure for reflection and improvement.

The manager provides steady and approachable leadership. Care staff told us they feel supported,
respected, and able to speak openly. The manager is visible throughout the home and responds
promptly when concerns arise. This promotes a culture where care staff feel valued and confident
to raise issues. People living at the home and relatives confirmed they can approach the manager
easily, and they express trust in the way the home is run.

Training and development systems are generally well managed. Care staff have access to relevant
courses through the local authority, and many are progressing with their care qualifications. Most
mandatory training is up to date, and the manager keeps an overview of areas that need
refreshing. Supervision mostly takes place every three months as expected, and these meetings
give staff an opportunity to reflect on their practice and identify any support they need. A small
number of supervisions had slipped slightly beyond the recommended timeframe, but this had no
negative impact on people’s well-being.

Some operational pressures remain. The absence of a dedicated on-site maintenance worker
means the manager must request repairs through central council processes, which can slow down
the completion of essential work. While these delays do not pose immediate risks to people, they
do place additional demand on the manager and can make it harder to maintain the environment to
the highest standard. The provider has acknowledged this and is taking steps to improve the speed
and communication of maintenance requests.

Morale amongst care staff is strong. Care staff describe the home as a positive place to work, with
good teamwork and reliable communication. People receive consistent support from a stable
workforce, and care staff work hard to meet their needs with dignity and respect. The provider has
developed the Denu Talent project to provide structured work experience placements that develop
skills, confidence and future employability within local care services.

Areas identified for improvement
Where we identify Areas for Improvement but we have not found outcomes for people to be at
immediate or significant risk, we discuss these with the provider. We expect the provider to take
action and we will follow this up at the next inspection.


Where we find outcomes for people require significant improvement and/or there is risk to
people’s well-being we identify areas for Priority Action. In these circumstances we issue a
Priority Action Notice(s) to the Provider, and they must take immediate steps to make
improvements. We will inspect again within six months to check improvements have been made
and outcomes for people have improved.


CIW has no areas for improvement identified following this inspection.


CIW has not issued any Priority action notices following this inspection.
