Summary:



 Meddyg Care Dementia Home - Rhyl is in a quiet residential area, close to the seafront and local
 cafes, shops and amenities.

 People have good well-being because staff know people well and support them to make everyday
 choices. People take part in activities that reflect their interests and benefit from meaningful
 engagement, positive relationships and a dementia-friendly environment. The service promotes the
 Welsh language and culture.

 People receive good care and support because detailed personal plans provide clear, risk-
 assessed guidance for timely, respectful and dignified support that meets their needs. Medication
 and infection prevention arrangements are safe. Relatives and professionals are positive about the
 manager and the quality of care provided in the home.

 People achieve good outcomes because the environment is clean, secure, and well maintained,
 with quiet areas, sensory features, and accessible outdoor space. There is ample equipment,
 supplies and facilities to meet people’s needs.

Findings:

        Well-being                           Good



 People have control over their day-to-day lives and experience positive well-being outcomes. Staff
 treat people with dignity and respect. They use warm, reassuring verbal communication and
 physical touch adapting their approach to each person’s needs and preferences. Staff offer choices
 about meals, drinks, activities and where people spend their time. They respond sensitively to each
 person’s lived reality, recognising their individuality and emotional needs while considering those of
 others nearby. This promotes people’s sense of control while managing risks to them and others.

 Staff use people’s histories and preferences to provide emotional support and meaningful
 occupation. We saw people dancing, singing, receiving nail care, and using sensory objects and
 pictures to engage in conversation. Staff recognise when people seek to continue familiar routines
 linked to their past social, family, or work lives. They respond sensitively by enabling them to take
 part in purposeful everyday tasks, such as collecting cups and laying tables. This personalised
 support keeps people living with dementia engaged in meaningful activities and promotes their
 physical and psychological well-being by helping them feel included. We saw people relaxing in
 positive moods and frequent warm interactions between staff and people they support, as well as
 between people themselves. Activity staff plan individual and group activities, while all staff support
 daily engagement.

 Relatives told us they feel people are safe in the home, and they can approach staff and the
 manager if there is a problem. Relatives were positive about the home and told us staff know their
 family member well, keep them informed and respond when needs change. The manager ensures
 staff make safeguarding referrals, seek healthcare advice, and inform families and relevant
 agencies in a timely way when the need arises.

 People are supported to maintain relationships. We saw affectionate, respectful interactions
 between people, and discrete support from staff to ensure people to spend time together but
 remain safe. Staff speak Welsh with people where this is preferred. There is bilingual and pictorial
 signage throughout the home to support people to orientate themselves in the home. Welsh culture
 and language are celebrated in the home with themed events, activities, and decorative touches
 around the home.

 People live in accommodation that supports their needs. The home offers quiet and social spaces,
 sensory and interactive artwork on the walls and accessible gardens. We saw photos of people
 enjoying the garden, and saw people supported to use all areas of the home during our inspection
 visit.

       Care & Support                       Good



People receive good care and support reflecting their assessed needs, preferences, and personal
histories. Strengths-based personal plans detail people’s health, communication, relationships,
interests, mobility, personal care, mental capacity, and professional advice. People and relatives
contribute to planning and reviews, while staff record healthcare advice and update plans when
needs change. Plans identify risks, including triggers for distress and expressive behaviours, and
guide staff on reassurance and prevention. Senior staff complete quality checklists each shift to
ensure care is recorded accurately and promptly. Experienced staff from the provider’s other
services are helping the team improve recording and safely embed its care model. New
personalised booklets capture each person’s preferences, life history, interests, and emotional
cues. Together with individual risk assessments and care plans, these will help staff recognise
distress early and provide consistent, individualised support that promotes calm and well-being.

We saw calm care tailored to people’s needs. Staffing levels and supervision of communal areas
were good during both visits. Staff respond to people without appearing rushed and work at an
appropriate pace for each individual. They use suitable moving and handling techniques, explain
each step, and maintain people’s dignity. One-to-one staff focus on the person supported, following
their lead and using preferred conversation topics, reminiscence, and tablet-based activities to
engage them meaningfully. At mealtimes, staff offer choices in ways suited to people’s
communication needs, encouraging independence to maintains people’s strengths and abilities.

People told us they feel safe. Relatives we spoke to confirmed this and were confident they could
raise anything with the manager and be heard. Records show staff report safeguarding concerns,
complete body maps when required, arrange professional assessments, and contact families,
police and healthcare professionals as needed. The provider analyses incident data to improve the
service. For example, they recently provided in-depth on-site training to further help staff recognise
early signs of distress and prevent escalation to expressive behaviours that may affect others.

Medication is managed safely. We saw nurses complete appropriate checks, remain with the
person until medicines are taken, record administration while with the person, and lock the trolley
when stepping away. Sampled medication administration records contained no gaps and confirmed
people received medicines as prescribed. Weekly stock checks and suitable best-interest decision-
making for covert medication are in place. Infection prevention arrangements are also good. Daily
cleaning staff follow checklists for bedrooms, communal areas and required deep cleans. The
home received a level 5 food hygiene rating in January 2026. We saw staff use personal protective
equipment correctly, while care plans address skin, wound and continence needs to reduce
infection risks.

       Environment                         Good



People live in a safe, clean, and dementia-friendly environment that supports their independence
and well-being. Communal areas, bathrooms, and corridors are clean, odour free, and free from
clutter. The provider has invested in the decoration and appearance of the home since the previous
inspection. Improvements include new furniture, secure door access, flooring, and sensory artwork.
Interactive sensory wall scenes provide familiar sounds of daily life and points of interest to engage
people living with dementia in reminiscence discussions. Bilingual and pictorial signs, contrasting
coloured toilet seats, good lighting and clear separation between areas help people to find their
way. Small seating areas allow people to rest as they move around the home. People can choose
between busier lounges and quieter spaces. We saw people using both. The garden has secure
boundaries, seating, covered areas, and raised planting. Further sensory planting is planned.

The provider manages improvement work in stages to reduce disruption. This is important because
some people move around the service during the day and night. During our visit we saw decorating
areas are safely separated while work takes place. Some carpeted areas remain stained, but the
provider is replacing these with suitable non-slip flooring in a staged manner to reduce disturbance
to residents. The home remained calm and accessible. Staff used music and sensory resources in
communal areas, which supported engagement and made spaces feel welcoming.

Equipment and safety systems are maintained. Fire certification is current and lifting equipment
receive required safety checks. Service records for hoists, standing aids, beds and pressure-
relieving equipment are in date. Staff test room sensors routinely, and provider audits electronic
call-bell information to ensure people are receiving timely support. There is CCTV in communal
areas and externally along with secure access to key areas of the home to keep people safe.
Leaders use CCTV to review incidents in communal areas, identify improvements and support
learning. Previous sensitivity issues with motion sensors in people’s rooms have been addressed to
reduce false alarms causing disturbance to people, whilst ensuring risks to people are reduced.
Maintenance staff complete repairs and environmental checks, and no unsafe equipment was
found. Management oversight of environmental audits had not always been consistent. Leaders
have restarted checks and allocated responsibilities between the manager and the provider’s
maintenance and health and safety teams.

       Leadership & Management               Good



Leadership and management support good outcomes for people. The manager is visible,
approachable, and knowledgeable about people’s needs; we saw them supporting people and
advising staff in communal areas. Relatives, staff, and visiting professionals spoke positively about
the manager’s professionalism, openness and responsiveness. Some health and social care
professionals who regularly visit the home said the team acts on their advice and manages
complex dementia care needs well. They also reported positive improvements in the service,
although this view was not shared consistently by all professionals we heard from during the
inspection.Staffing levels and deployment across the home meet people’s needs. During both
visits, staff were always visible in communal areas and one-to-one staff focused on the person
supported. Allocation sheets identify staff deployment by floor, team leaders and clinical
responsibility for each shift. We saw staff assist across all floors, with the manager and nurses
providing additional support at busy times. The manager is increasing nurses’ time on the floor to
strengthen clinical leadership and support care staff. Sampled rotas showed consistent staffing
across days and nights, including extra staff support on one night. Service leaders also provided
significant on-site support during a recent period when people’s dementia-related support needs
increased.

Staff receive online, face-to-face and specialist training from internal and external trainers that
meets people’s needs. This covers dementia care, de-escalation, moving and handling, falls,
medication, infection prevention and clinical topics for nurses. Regular supervision and annual
appraisals support staff well-being and development. Staff told us they feel well supported and get
the training they need to support people well. Safe recruitment and vetting arrangements include
induction training, shadow shifts and probationary periods, alongside ongoing monitoring of
professional registration, right-to-work and disclosure checks. Agency care staff are occasionally
used during the day. The provider promotes continuity and quality by requesting familiar agency
staff, checking their vetting and qualifications, and helping them learn about people they are
supporting alongside knowledgeable permanent staff.

Governance systems are active. The responsible individual (RI) visits regularly and attends monthly
management and quality meetings, where emerging issues and resulting improvement actions are
reviewed. Quality reviews and audits cover incidents, safeguarding, medication, staffing, training
and the environment. Records show leaders identify concerns and agree actions. They recognise
quality-assurance records can be strengthened to show how decisions, incidents and feedback
inform improvement. Revised reviews, including unannounced mock inspections, are being
implemented to monitor progress and whether actions are effective and sustained.

Areas identified for improvement
Where we identify Areas for Improvement but we have not found outcomes for people to be at
immediate or significant risk, we discuss these with the provider. We expect the provider to take
action and we will follow this up at the next inspection.


Where we find outcomes for people require significant improvement and/or there is risk to
people’s well-being we identify areas for Priority Action. In these circumstances we issue a
Priority Action Notice(s) to the Provider, and they must take immediate steps to make
improvements. We will inspect again within six months to check improvements have been made
and outcomes for people have improved.


CIW has no areas for improvement identified following this inspection.


CIW has not issued any Priority action notices following this inspection.
