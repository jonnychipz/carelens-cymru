Summary:


 Warrendale Cottage is a residential care home on the outskirts of Broughton village. It is close to
 local amenities, shops, and to the city of Chester. The service benefits from a beautifully
 maintained, and fully accessible garden for people to enjoy. People experience good wellbeing
 outcomes with a range of activities on offer. Care staff are committed to providing good quality care
 and support. They take time to get to know people and their individual needs, following their
 personal plans to ensure care is delivered in line with people’s preferences.

 People live in a comfortable and homely environment. It is well maintained to ensure the building is
 safe and secure. Leadership and management of the service have a solid foundation, with an
 ongoing commitment to continuous improvement. The responsible individual (RI) is regularly
 present in the service, to support the care staff and oversee the smooth running of the service.

Findings:

        Well-being                           Good





 People living at the service experience a good level of well-being, with evidence that they are
 supported to have a voice, choice, and control over their daily lives. During the inspection, people
 were observed engaging positively with one another and staff, socialising both at mealtimes and
 during activities. Mealtimes were seen to be relaxed and sociable occasions, with people
 describing a sense of community, for example referring to themselves as “ladies who lunch,”
 indicating a culture that promotes dignity and enjoyment.

 The manager and care staff ensure there is a strong emphasis on meaningful activity, with a range
 of opportunities available to suit different preferences. These include intergenerational activities,
 exercise sessions, and access to the gardens for walking and relaxing. There are visits from clergy
 with communion held at the home every four weeks. On the day of inspection, nursery children
 visited the service, and we saw people engaging naturally with them, and the pleasure this brought
 to both people and visiting children. People also contribute to the delivery of activities, for example
 leading sessions like chair exercises or playing the piano during communal events. This
 demonstrates a culture where people are not passive recipients of care but are encouraged to
 maintain their identities and share interests.

 People and relatives, we spoke with reported good levels of satisfaction, with comments describing
 the environment as “homely” and staff as “kind.” One person told us, “It feels like being at home”
 and another commented “It is very comfortable here.” Relatives noted improvements in people’s
 well-being since their admission. Visiting arrangements were reported to be flexible, supporting
 people to maintain relationships that are important to them. There are private spaces available for
 people to meet with their visitors.

 People are supported to feel safe and protected, with appropriate systems and safeguards in place
 where required. People who lack capacity to consent to their care and accommodation
 arrangements have Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards in place. This means they have access to a
 representative to ensure their voice is heard and uphold their rights. Individuals confirmed they feel
 able to raise concerns with care staff and management.

 The environment further supports well-being, with accessible outdoor areas, including gardens and
 seating spaces, which people actively use. We saw people accessing the grounds for walks, and
 there is a patio area besides the new “chicken hotel” where people can sit and watch the chickens.
 People told us they look forward to attending the summer barbeques in the garden.

       Care & Support                       Good





People receive care and support that is person-centred and responsive to their needs. There are
systems in place to identify and respond to changing needs. Care plans and risk assessments were
available for individuals and reflected personal preferences, health conditions and required
interventions. Personal plans contain a good level of detail about people’s preferences and
routines. Care staff follow these to ensure care is delivered in line with individual preferences.
Personal plans and risk assessments are reviewed regularly and updated with any changes.

The manager has established good relationships with professionals such as GPs, dietitians and
district nurses contributing to people’s care. They are working to strengthen communication
between visiting professionals and the staff team. This helps ensure that health needs are
appropriately managed. Safeguarding processes are also well embedded, with incidents reported
and investigated appropriately, and staff demonstrating confidence in raising concerns.

Medication practices were observed to be safe and person-centred. Care staff administering
medication explained procedures to people and took time to support them appropriately.
Medication administration records were completed correctly, and staff had received relevant
training and competency assessments. Any discrepancies in medication records are investigated
with actions put in place to reduce the risk of any recurrence.

Care staff deliver care and support in line with people’s personal plans. There are monitoring
records kept in areas such as food and fluid charts to record people’s intake where needed. These
monitoring systems are being strengthened by the service to ensure there is consistency in the
record keeping, and care staff are clear about when to escalate any issues or concerns. There are
systems to monitor and record changes in people’s needs. The service aims to meet increasing
needs through joint working with external professionals enabling them to avoid disruption in
people’s lives, by delivering end of life care. Some care staff have completed specialist end of life
care training.

       Environment                         Good





People live in a safe, clean, and homely environment that supports people’s comfort,
independence, and overall well-being. The premises were observed to be tidy, well-maintained,
and personalised, with people able to decorate their rooms and spend time either privately or in
communal areas according to their preferences. This promotes both autonomy and a sense of
belonging. There are a variety of communal spaces available, including quiet areas and social
environments, enabling people to choose how and where they spend their time. The environment
supports social interaction while also respecting individuals’ need for privacy. In addition, there are
dedicated areas for visits, ensuring people can meet with relatives and professionals in a
confidential and comfortable setting. Outdoor spaces are a notable strength, with accessible and
well-used gardens which enhance people’s day-day experience. Features such as the summer
house, a barbeque area and comfortable seating provide opportunities for leisure, stimulation, and
connection with nature. People were observed using these spaces, demonstrating their value in
supporting well-being. They reported they enjoy sitting outside and being able to watch the
chickens. One person told us the service has “fantastic gardens”.

The service provider ensures appropriate health and safety measures are in place, including
regular servicing of equipment such as fire systems, electrical installations and lifting equipment.
Infection prevention and control (IPC) procedures are in place, with routine audits completed.
Safety features such as window restrictors and radiator covers contribute to risk reduction. The
manager completes regular environmental and IPC checks to ensure the safety of the home and
minimise the risk of infection. The building is safe and secure. There is external CCTV in use and a
signing in book for visitors. We were unable to enter the premises without making ourselves known
to staff and signing in. There is an internal system of CCTV cameras in communal areas, which we
were assured are not in use, to protect people’s rights to privacy.

       Leadership & Management               Good





The manager and RI ensure robust recruitment practices are followed, and staff receive induction,
supervision, and appraisal, supporting safe and competent practice. Care staff are required to
complete disclosure and barring service (DBS) checks and to register with Social Care Wales, the
workforce regulator. The service supports staff development, with training opportunities and
identified roles such as champions in areas like IPC and end-of-life care. Care staff are due to
receive specialist training in catheter care. They described good teamwork and morale, reinforced
by recognition and celebratory events. They told us they feel supported, valued, and able to raise
concerns. Management is visible and responsive, with the manager and RI providing oversight and
support to the team. Care staff told us they have a good team, and it is “like a family”.


The manager completes regular audits to monitor the service. These include audits of medication
and infection control. They also monitor any accidents and incidents, with falls and accidents
recorded and reviewed to ensure risks are reduced. Notifications are submitted appropriately,
demonstrating awareness of regulatory responsibilities. The RI completes formal three-monthly
visits to the service to ensure it is running smoothly. They speak to people and staff for feedback
and review a selection of records. They also undertake spot checks during the night shift, to ensure
good continuity of care is maintained. Quality of care reports are produced every six months,
providing an overview of what is working well and considering where improvements can be made.
These would benefit from the consideration of any patterns or trends, for safeguarding issues,
complaints, and incidents. The manager and RI plan to review the quality-of-care reports
incorporating the updated guidance from Care Inspectorate Wales (CIW).

The RI has put in place policies and procedures which care staff have access too. They are
reviewed regularly to ensure they remain up to date. As we considered CCTV during this
inspection, they intend to review the CCTV policy to ensure there is clarity around the areas in
which it is in use and the purpose for which it is used.

The provider ensures good governance structures are in place for the service and is building upon
this with plans for continuous improvement. The RI invests in the service, to ensure it is well
maintained. They listen to people’s feedback on suggested improvements, and as a result made
the decision to keep some chickens after hatching last year, creating a chicken hutch with viewing
area in the garden.

Areas identified for improvement
Where we identify Areas for Improvement but we have not found outcomes for people to be at
immediate or significant risk, we discuss these with the provider. We expect the provider to take
action and we will follow this up at the next inspection.


Where we find outcomes for people require significant improvement and/or there is risk to
people’s well-being we identify areas for Priority Action. In these circumstances we issue a
Priority Action Notice(s) to the Provider, and they must take immediate steps to make
improvements. We will inspect again within six months to check improvements have been made
and outcomes for people have improved.


CIW has no areas for improvement identified following this inspection.


CIW has not issued any Priority action notices following this inspection.
