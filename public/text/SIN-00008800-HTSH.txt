Summary:

 Situated in small community in the centre of Cardiff, St Albans provides specialist, person-centred
 nursing support for people over the age of eighteen, living with Dementia, Complex Mental Health
 Needs,  Huntington’s Disease, Acquired Brain  Injury,  Neurological  Conditions, and Learning
 Disabilities.

 Wellbeing is rated good because most people experience positive wellbeing outcomes and benefit
 from a  caring,  person-centred  approach  which  promotes  meaningful  lives,  independence,
 relationships and participation. Whilst improvements are needed to strengthen involvement in care
 planning and ensure greater consistency in some aspects of care delivery, people are generally
 supported to achieve outcomes that matter to them and enjoy a good quality of life.

 Care and support require improvement because people are not always meaningfully involved in
 care planning and review processes. Personal plans and associated records are not always

accurate, up to date and do not clearly demonstrate how care is delivered in accordance with
individuals' wishes, aspirations, risks and specialist needs.

Environment is rated good as people benefit from a welcoming and well-resourced environment
that supports wellbeing, activity and independence. The provider has demonstrated ongoing
investment and improvement in the physical environment.

Leadership and management require significant improvement because people do not consistently
benefit from effective operational leadership, governance, and workforce arrangements to promote
safe, high-quality care and support. People are not routinely involved in the development of their
initial personal plans or in subsequent reviews, limiting opportunities to ensure care reflects their
needs, preferences, and changing circumstances. As a result, there is an increased risk that care
and support may not be appropriate, effective, or responsive to individual needs.

Findings:

        Well-being                           Good



 People are treated with dignity and respect. People tell us they are supported to live lives which are
 meaningful to them. Staff take time to understand who they are, what matters to them and what
 they want to achieve. We saw care staff demonstrating an outstanding commitment to helping
 people achieve their personal aspirations and maintain lifelong interests. One person has fulfilled a
 lifelong ambition through a personalised visit to Liverpool's Anfield Stadium. Another was enabled
 to reconnect with his passion for golf through visits to a local golf club, the purchase of new
 equipment and attendance at the Ryder Cup. These exceptional examples show how staff go
 beyond routine care to create meaningful opportunities, promote positive risk-taking and support
 people to lead fulfilling lives rich in purpose, achievement and personal satisfaction.

 Families describe positive examples of staff supporting important life events and personal
 outcomes. One relative said, “staff supported my mother after the death of her husband, helping
 her attend his funeral and purchasing a new dress for the occasion". Another described a
 personalised wedding anniversary celebration, adding, “I felt very touched by this gesture as it
 made the occasion special for us both."

 The provider demonstrates a strong commitment to supporting people's spiritual, cultural and
 personal wishes. Strong partnerships with families, faith groups and community organisations
 ensure people's health, religious beliefs and end-of-life preferences are respected, promoted and
 fulfilled in ways which are highly meaningful to them.

 We found people have a genuine voice in shaping life within the home. Care staff actively listen
 and respond to people's ideas, resulting in meaningful opportunities such as themed community
 events and a planned group holiday to Bluestone.

 Relationships are well supported, with families playing an active and valued role in the life of the
 home. One relative told us, “Staff try to include me in activities and events,".

 People are encouraged to maintain their independence wherever possible. One person told us, "I
 am very independent", explaining she prepares her own breakfast and manages her personal care
 independently.

 People tell us they feel safe, supported and able to exercise choice and control over their lives.
 Accessible information, advocacy support and clear guidance on how to raise concerns are
 available in formats to meet individual communication needs.

       Care & Support            Requires Improvement



People benefit from a provider who is committed to supporting individual preferences, interests and
aspirations. We saw positive examples of personalised support with people being helped to
achieve outcomes that are important to them. We observed positive interactions between staff and
people living at the home, with regular staff who know people well.

Personal plans reflect a range of specialist interventions to meet people's health and well-being
needs, including support with mobility, communication, nutrition, dementia and behavioural support.
However, the provider has not demonstrated whether all staff are aware of and consistently follow
the required care instructions in personal plans, particularly in relation to positive behavioural
support plans.

While we saw evidence that people's needs have been assessed and appropriate care and support
has been planned, improvements are needed to strengthen care monitoring and record keeping.
We identified gaps in records relating to pressure care, behavioural support, PEG care, continence
management and skin integrity monitoring. Records do not consistently demonstrate that planned
interventions have been delivered or reviewed in line with people's assessed needs.
Documentation relating to pressure care does not always evidence that repositioning has taken
place as required. Behaviour support records do not clearly show whether agreed strategies have
been followed. Guidance within records is not always consistent, making it difficult to demonstrate
that care is being delivered in line with agreed plans. Outcomes for people require improvement
because records relating to individuals are not accurate or up to date and we expect the provider to
make improvements.

People receive access to appropriate health care. We found evidence of strong multidisciplinary
working through access to in-house specialists, including Occupational Therapists, Speech and
Language Therapists, Psychologists and Positive Behaviour Support practitioners. The provider
has effective partnerships with external healthcare professionals. Families provide positive
examples of communication and collaborative working between staff and professionals, particularly
in relation to managing complex health conditions, swallowing difficulties and dietary requirements.
We found that specialist advice is generally sought in a timely manner.

Medication systems are in place and people generally receive the support they need with their
medicines. However, we identified some inconsistencies in medication recording and monitoring,
particularly in relation to behavioural support medication. These issues reduce assurances that
medicines are always managed, monitored and documented consistently.

The service demonstrates good infection prevention and control arrangements. The home holds a
Food Hygiene Rating of 5 and we observed staff following appropriate infection control procedures
throughout the inspection. Care staff wear suitable personal protective equipment (PPE), practise

       Environment                         Good



People benefit from a comfortable, welcoming and well-maintained environment. There are a range
of communal spaces where people can relax, socialise and take part in meaningful activities. We
saw a newly developed conservatory for relaxation and social engagement. Dedicated dining and
lounge areas, supporting restaurant-style dining, family visits and group activities.

Individual bedrooms are personalised to reflect people’s interests, preferences and identities.
Residents are supported to choose décor and furnishings, including through the use of iPads.
Rooms reflect individual choice and create a homely environment. We saw personalised bedrooms
featuring favourite football teams, family photographs and personal interests. One recently
refurbished room is designed around the person’s preferred football club colours. Picture boards
outside bedrooms provide staff and visitors with information about people's interests and
preferences, supporting conversation.

People have access to attractive outdoor spaces promoting relaxation, social interaction and
meaningful activity. Gardens contain raised flower and vegetable beds, sensory plants, a water
feature and several seating areas for people and visitors. We saw a greenhouse and vegetable
plots growing carrots, tomatoes and pumpkins. People are actively involved in gardening projects,
with herbs grown and used in meal preparation. Privacy screening helps create a comfortable and
secluded environment.

The home's facilities further support people's wellbeing and quality of life. An in-house beauty room
has been developed following consultation with residents. It provides access to hairdressing and
personal grooming facilities within an environment designed to resemble a professional salon. The
provider continues to invest in improvements

Information throughout the home is presented in an accessible and organised way. Noticeboards
display a range of information including activities, wellbeing information, and upcoming events. A
digital memory display in the main corridor showcases photographs of people enjoying holidays,
community outings, celebrations and activities. This helps promote reminiscence, conversation and
a welcoming atmosphere.

The provider continues to improve accessibility, privacy and safety. Automatic sliding entrance
doors have been installed to enhance accessibility and independence. We found specialist bathing
facilities on each floor, including adapted bathrooms with integrated hoists and accessible wet room
ensuites. A range of specialist equipment is available to support people's mobility needs. The
home's wide corridors and doorways enable safe movement throughout the building.

                                    Requires Significant
       Leadership & Management
                                       Improvement


People are supported by a committed staff team who are motivated to provide caring and
compassionate support. We observed positive leadership practices, including regular daily
handover meetings which promote effective communication, information sharing and discussion of
concerns across the service.

The provider has a range of governance and quality assurance systems in place, including regular
responsible individual (RI) visits, governance meetings, audits and observations of care. RI
oversight demonstrates a good understanding of the service, with reports identifying both strengths
and areas for improvement, supported by action planning to drive progress. Environmental audits
and the annual quality review show a reflective approach to monitoring and improving service
quality. The provider seeks feedback from relatives and staff. Families report improvements in
communication and involvement. Positive feedback highlights caring and supportive care staff who
are committed to people's wellbeing

However, governance systems are not consistently effective in identifying, addressing and
sustaining improvements. Although audit and monitoring processes are in place, they have not
identified concerns found during the inspection, including gaps in the oversight of care records, risk
assessments and outcomes for people.

Staff feedback highlights concerns about organisational culture; with some reporting they lack
confidence in raising concerns with management. Staffing remains a significant challenge for the
service. Although care staff report staffing levels have improved recently and recruitment activity is
ongoing, concerns about vacancies, staff retention, reliance on agency workers and staff ‘burnout’
remain. Stakeholders tell us high use of agency staff affects consistency of care. Care staff said
workload pressures limit their ability to spend meaningful time with people or complete
documentation to the standard expected.

There is an extensive training programme and examples of specialist training are available.
However, significant gaps remain in specialist training provision and skill mix. The service
predominantly supports people with mental health needs, yet no Registered Mental Health Nurses
(RMNs) are employed, and not all staff have received dedicated mental health training. Concerns
were also identified regarding agency staff induction, competency assurance and some medication
management practices. These matters mean people are not always receiving support from staff
with the necessary specialist knowledge and expertise.

Supervision arrangements are established and conducted regularly; however, records reviewed do
not consistently demonstrate reflective practice, management oversight or clear action planning.

People are exposed to an increased risk of poor outcomes because the provider has not

established sufficiently effective governance, oversight and quality assurance arrangements to
consistently monitor, evaluate and improve the quality and safety of care. People are not involved
in support planning or subsequent reviews, further weakening quality assurance processes.
Immediate action is required to address these significant shortcomings.

Areas identified for improvement
Where we identify Areas for Improvement but we have not found outcomes for people to be at
immediate or significant risk, we discuss these with the provider. We expect the provider to take
action and we will follow this up at the next inspection.


Where we find outcomes for people require significant improvement and/or there is risk to
people’s well-being we identify areas for Priority Action. In these circumstances we issue a
Priority Action Notice(s) to the Provider, and they must take immediate steps to make
improvements. We will inspect again within six months to check improvements have been made
and outcomes for people have improved.
